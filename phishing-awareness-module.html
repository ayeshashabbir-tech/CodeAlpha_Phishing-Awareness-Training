    Lesson 1

    One click is all an attacker needs

    Phishing is not a technology problem that firewalls solve. It is a message, written by a person, designed to make you act before you think.

    Most serious breaches still start with a message: an email, a text, a phone call, a chat request, a QR code on a parking meter. The attacker is not breaking encryption. They are asking you politely, and urgently, to hand over something you already have access to — a password, a one-time code, an approval, a payment.

    That is why you matter here. Security tools filter the obvious attempts; the ones that reach your inbox are the ones that got through. You are not the weakest link. You are the last checkpoint, and the only one that can notice something feels wrong.

    What phishing is trying to get

    • Credentials — your username, password, and the six-digit code that protects them.
    • Money — an invoice paid to a new bank account, a gift-card purchase, a wire transfer.
    • A foothold — an attachment or a link that installs something, so the attacker can move through the network at their own pace.
    • Information — an org chart, a supplier list, a travel schedule. Dull on its own, and perfect fuel for the next, more convincing message.
    A note on spelling mistakes. Old training said to look for bad grammar. That signal has largely gone: attackers now write fluent, on-brand copy in any language. Judge the request and the sender, not the prose.

    The four shapes it arrives in

    • Bulk phishing — the same message to millions, hoping for a small percentage.
    • Spear phishing — written for you, using your job title, your manager's name, a project you posted about.
    • Whaling — aimed at executives and finance staff, where a single approval is worth millions.
    • Business email compromise — no malicious link at all, just a believable request from a real or spoofed colleague, supplier, or lawyer.

    The checklist behind those clues

    • Sender domain — read the part after the @, character by character. A display name is free to set; a domain has to be bought.
    • Reply-To — if replies go somewhere other than the sender, someone wants your answer routed elsewhere.
    • Addressing — a real provider knows your name and writes to you alone.
    • A deadline — countdowns exist to stop you checking.
    • A threat — loss of access, loss of files, loss of pay. Fear shortens thinking.
    • The link's real domain — everything before the first single slash is what counts.
    • The ask itself — no legitimate provider asks you to confirm a password by email. Not once, not ever.

    Lesson 4

    Fake websites and the links that lead there

    A cloned login page is trivial to build. Modern kits copy the branding, the fonts, the password-reset flow, and even relay your one-time code to the real site in real time. Your defence is the address bar, read carefully, before you type anything.

    How to read a web address

    Find //, then read forward to the first single slash. The last two labels before that slash are the real owner of the site. Everything else — subdomains, folders, parameters — is decoration the attacker controls.

    Address bar anatomy

    https://login.account-verify.co/microsoft/secure?id=88123

    Owner of this site: account-verify.co. The word "login" is a subdomain anyone can create, and "/microsoft/" is just a folder name.

    Seven tricks, and what gives them away

    Subdomain padding

    paypal.com.secure-login.xyz is owned by secure-login.xyz. Read right to left.

    Typosquatting

    rnicrosoft.com uses r-n to imitate m. Also doubled letters and swapped vowels.

    Lookalike characters

    Cyrillic and Greek letters can render as Latin ones. A domain starting xn-- in the address bar is a red flag.

    Wrong suffix

    The brand you know is .com, the link is .co, .cm, or .support.

    Shorteners and QR codes

    Both hide the destination completely. Expand a short link before opening; treat unexpected QR codes as untrusted links.

    The padlock proves nothing

    HTTPS means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is honest. Phishing pages get free certificates too.

    Silent password manager

    Your password manager matches on exact domain. If it will not autofill a site you use daily, believe the tool, not the page.

    Lesson 5

    The social engineering playbook

    Phishing works on people, not machines. Every campaign leans on a small set of psychological levers. Once you can name the lever being pulled, the message loses most of its power. Select a tactic to hear how it sounds in practice.

    Beyond email

    • Vishing — a phone call from "IT" or "the bank", often after an email to make it credible. Voice cloning has made a familiar voice worthless as proof.
    • Smishing — texts about parcels, tolls, tax refunds, or a manager's "new number".
    • Quishing — a QR code in a PDF, poster, or parking meter that carries the link past email filters and onto your personal phone.
    • MFA fatigue — repeated push notifications at 2am until you approve one to make them stop. Never approve a prompt you did not trigger.
    • Consent phishing — a real login page, followed by a request to grant an app permission to read your mail. No password stolen, full access given.
    • Callback phishing — an invoice for something you did not buy, with a number to call and dispute it. The number is theirs.
    • Baiting and tailgating — a USB stick left in the car park, or a friendly stranger with full hands at a badge-controlled door.
    The tell that survives every disguise. Almost every attack ends with one of three requests: send money, reveal a credential or code, or open this thing. Any message combining urgency with one of those three deserves a second channel of verification, no matter who appears to have sent it.

    Lesson 7

    Habits that stop it

    Knowing the theory does not protect anyone. These eight habits do, because they work even when the message is convincing and you are busy.

    Verify on a second channel

    Unexpected payment or credential request? Call back on a number you already had — from the intranet or a previous invoice, never from the message.

    Never follow a link to log in

    Open a new tab and type the address yourself, or use your own bookmark. This one habit defeats most credential phishing.

    Let the password manager decide

    Store every work login in it and stop typing passwords by hand. It refuses to autofill lookalike domains, which makes it a detector as much as a vault.

    Use phishing-resistant MFA

    Passkeys and hardware security keys are tied to the real domain, so a cloned page cannot use them. Prefer them over SMS and app codes.

    Treat codes like passwords

    No colleague, bank, or helpdesk will ever need your one-time code. Reading it aloud hands over your account.

    Slow down on urgency

    Make deadline pressure your trigger to pause rather than hurry. Nothing legitimate collapses because you took ten minutes to check.

    Report, do not just delete

    Use the report button. Deleting protects you alone; reporting lets security pull the same message from everyone else's inbox.

    Keep work and personal separate

    Do not forward work mail to personal accounts, and be sparing with what you publish about your role — it is the raw material for spear phishing.

    If you are the finance approver. Any change to supplier bank details is a verification event, always, with no exceptions for urgency or seniority. Call the supplier on the number from the signed contract and confirm the change aloud.

    Lesson 8

    You clicked. Now what?

    People who report quickly limit the damage. People who stay quiet out of embarrassment turn a small incident into a large one. Speed matters far more than blame.

    1. Stop interactingClose the page. Do not enter anything else, do not approve any further prompt, and do not reply to the message.
    2. Report it immediatelyContact your security team or helpdesk right away, even if you only suspect it, and even if it is the middle of the night. Keep the original message so they can trace it.
    3. Change the password from a device you trustIf you entered credentials, change that password and any other account where you reused it. Use a different device if you think the first one is compromised.
    4. Sign out everywhereRevoke active sessions and check for any app permissions, forwarding rules, or recovery addresses added to your account. Attackers set those up within minutes.
    5. Disconnect if something was openedIf you opened an attachment or downloaded a file, disconnect from the network and let IT examine the device rather than running a scan and assuming it is clean.
    6. Involve the bank earlyIf money moved, tell your finance team and the bank at once. Wire transfers can sometimes be recalled within hours, rarely after that.
    7. Watch for the follow-upA successful attack is often followed by a second, more personalised one. Expect it, and warn the colleagues most likely to be targeted next.
    For managers. The reporting rate matters more than the click rate. If reporting a mistake is treated as a good day's work rather than a failure, you find out in minutes instead of months.
